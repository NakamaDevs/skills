# Write colleague notes

Write something the author could comfortably send to the person who needs it.
The goal is a useful conversation on the page, not a polished report about that
conversation. Changing punctuation alone is not enough.

## Start with the reader

Use the conversation and document to identify who is speaking, who is reading,
and what the reader needs to answer or do. Do not ask again when this is clear.
Preserve the document's language and the author's level of formality.

Lead with the actual situation: what we know, what we think, and what we need
to find out. Address a known recipient directly when it fits. Use “I” or “we”
when drafting in the author's voice, but only for views or actions supported
by the source. Do not invent interviews, experience, decisions, or promises.

For example, “I think we may have a few people worth revisiting” is appropriate
when the author has said that. It is not permission to invent a candidate pool.

## Rewrite the structure as well as the sentences

- Use a short, ordinary title: “Platform role questions” or “Notes for Friday.”
  Avoid stacked labels such as “Technical Coverage Review & Discovery Assessment.”
- Name headings after the subject: “Network engineer,” “A few questions,” or
  “What we need next.” Avoid repeating “scope and follow-up questions” for each role.
- Put a short explanation before the questions when it helps the reader answer
  them. Combine repeated background into that paragraph.
- Remove executive summaries, methodology sections, coverage summaries, and
  closing recaps when they repeat what the reader just read. Keep any unique
  facts or decisions they contain.
- Keep headings and bullets where they make the document easier to use. Do not
  flatten a useful document into a wall of prose or force it into a fixed template.
- Keep source links available without turning every paragraph into an evidence
  report. For informal notes, descriptive links near the claim or a short links
  section usually work. Preserve required citations and exact quotations.

## Sound like a colleague

Use direct verbs, familiar words, and natural contractions where they fit.
Mix short and medium sentences. Do not make every sentence the same length or
fragment the prose just to sound simple. Follow any applicable language standard
without adding unnecessary formality.

Replace abstract labels with the concrete thought behind them:

- “Coverage remains unconfirmed” becomes “We still need to check whether we have
  someone with that experience and availability.”
- “Validate candidate alignment against the confirmed scope” becomes “Check
  whether they've done this kind of work before.”
- “Suggested candidate evidence after scope confirmation” becomes “Once we know
  that, I'd ask candidates to walk through a similar project.”

Avoid marketing claims, inflated adjectives, stock transitions, and commentary
about the document itself. Phrases such as “unlock value,” “robust assessment,”
“high-confidence fit,” and “this review focuses on” usually hide a simpler point.
If one is a meaningful customer quote, preserve its attribution and ask what it
means in practice instead of presenting it as your own conclusion.

Do not use em dashes in newly written prose. Use a period, comma, colon, or
parentheses as appropriate. Preserve exact source quotations, identifiers, and
technical strings even when they contain one. Do not replace every em dash with
a semicolon or another repetitive punctuation habit.

Do not add fake informality: slang, jokes, deliberate errors, filler, or forced
friendliness. Do not promise that the result will evade AI detection.

## Ask questions someone can answer

Ask about the actual work, tools, constraints, ownership, and timing. Put the
questions that affect the next decision first. Group closely related questions,
but avoid long sentences asking for five unrelated things.

Prefer “Which products and versions do they use?” to “What is the technology
landscape?” Prefer “Will this person design it or work from an existing plan?”
to “Clarify the ownership model.” Keep technical terms that make the question
precise; do not explain familiar concepts as if selling a service.

Do not add an exhaustive checklist just because the topic could support one.
Keep the scope the user chose. A request about staffing coverage does not need
pricing, margin analysis, or funding questions unless the user asks for them.

## Keep the facts intact

Keep the distinction between possible, checked, available, agreed, and completed.
Make uncertainty sound natural, not less certain. “We may have a few people to
revisit” must not become “We can cover this role.”

Preserve important qualifications, technical requirements, names, dates, versions,
numbers, links, and ownership. State a necessary caveat once where it matters;
remove repeated disclaimers, not the underlying limitation. Do not silently
resolve conflicting source statements. Turn the conflict into a useful question.

When editing a shared document, read its current contents first. Respect edits
made since the previous draft. Keep comments, links, tables, and native structure
unless changing them is part of the request. Use the appropriate document tools
and verify the saved result. This skill controls writing, not access or delivery:
it does not authorize sending messages, sharing files, or publishing externally.

## A few examples

These are generic examples, not text to copy into every document.

**Opening**

Before: “The opportunity is ready for focused discovery. Technical staffing
coverage is not yet established.”

After: “We need a little more detail before we can say whether we have someone
for this. A short job description would help, or we could talk through the work
with the team.”

**A role note**

Before: “Review: the role requires clarification of architectural ownership
prior to candidate qualification.”

After: “This sounds like a hands-on engineering role. Will they work from an
existing design, or do they need to design it themselves?”

**An honest handoff**

Before: “Successful local validation establishes readiness for downstream
acceptance activities.”

After: “The local checks passed. We still need to try it in the shared environment
before we call it ready.”

## Finish with one quick pass

Read it as if you were the recipient. Can you tell what the author thinks and
what you need to answer? Does it sound like a person speaking to you, or a report
describing a process? Remove repetition and check that no facts or uncertainty
were lost. Check the title and headings too, not just the body.

Return the revised text or update the requested document. A short note about
the changes is enough; do not append a lengthy explanation of the rewrite.
